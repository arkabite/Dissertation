What is EEG?
assistant
General knowledge
General neuroscience background

EEG (electroencephalography) is a non‑invasive technique for recording the brain’s electrical activity. Electrodes are placed on the scalp, and the tiny voltage fluctuations generated by neuronal populations—particularly the synchronous firing of pyramidal cells in the cortex—are measured over time. The resulting time‑series signals capture oscillatory activity across a range of frequency bands (delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma) that are commonly correlated with different cognitive states such as attention, working memory, and fatigue. Because EEG provides high temporal resolution, it is widely used in studies of mental workload, memory tasks, and brain–computer interfacing. The signals are typically multi‑channel, requiring preprocessing (filtering, artefact removal, normalisation) before they can be used for feature extraction and classification in computational models.

What is the hemodynamic response function?
assistant
General knowledge
General neuroscience background

The hemodynamic response function (HRF) is a key concept in non‑invasive neuroimaging, linking neuronal activity to changes in cerebral blood flow and oxygenation that are observed by techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and functional near‑infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS). When a population of neurons becomes active, metabolic demand increases and the brain locally augments blood flow, oxygen delivery, and hemoglobin concentration. The HRF describes the typical time course of those haemodynamic changes following a brief pulse of neural activity.

Key features of a canonical HRF are:

Latency – the delay between neural activation and the onset of the haemodynamic signal, usually around 2–3 s in cortical grey matter.
Peak – the maximum (often a positive change in oxygenated haemoglobin or BOLD signal) around 4–6 s after stimulus onset.
Return to baseline – the gradual decline back to resting levels, usually over 8–15 s.
Form – often approximated by a gamma‑function or a sum of gamma functions, sometimes with a small undershoot (a dip below baseline) before the final return.
In practice, the HRF is treated as a convolution kernel: a given stimulus sequence is mathematically “blended” with the HRF to predict the measured haemodynamic signal. Accurate modelling of the HRF is essential for detecting task‑evoked responses, for separating overlapping events, and for any statistical analysis that assumes linearity between neural activity and the measured signal.

Although the canonical HRF works well for many cortical applications, actual haemodynamic dynamics can vary with brain region, age, vascular health, and the type of tissue (for example, white matter may show a delayed or reduced HRF). An automatically retrieved, unverified study by Li et al. (2019) reported task‑specific HRFs in white‑matter tracts with reduced magnitude and delayed onset compared to grey matter, highlighting that the HRF can differ across tissue types.

Because fNIRS measures changes in oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated (HbR) haemoglobin, accurate estimation of the HRF (and its possible variation) is crucial for interpreting cognitive workload or working‑memory tasks and for training any model that uses these signals as input.

What is neurovascular coupling?
assistant
General knowledge
General neuroscience background

Neurovascular coupling refers to the physiological link between local neural activity and regional changes in cerebral blood flow (CBF). When neurons are active they consume oxygen and glucose, creating a metabolic demand that signals the neurovascular system to increase blood delivery. This signaling involves a coordinated exchange of signals between neurons, astrocytes (glial cells that contact synapses and blood vessels), and vascular cells such as endothelial cells and smooth muscle cells. The result is vasodilation of capillaries and arterioles supplying the active area, ensuring that oxygenated haemoglobin (HbO) rises while deoxygenated haemoglobin (HbR) falls. This haemodynamic response is the basis for functional brain imaging techniques such as fNIRS and fMRI. An automatically retrieved, unverified paper by Girouard and Iadecola (2005) summarizes how neurons and glia initiate the vasodilatory cascade that ultimately shapes CBF changes.

Explain the n-back task
assistant
General knowledge
General neuroscience background

The n‑back task is a classic paradigm used to probe working‑memory load. In a typical version, a continuous stream of stimuli (e.g., letters, digits, or spatial cues) is presented at a fixed pace. Participants are asked to indicate when the current stimulus matches the one presented n items earlier. For example, in a 1‑back task the match is one item back, whereas a 2‑back or 3‑back task requires remembering and comparing to two or three items earlier, respectively.

Because the task demands continuous serial rehearsal, attention, and the updating of a short‑term memory buffer, the cognitive load rises systematically with increasing n. This load‑sensitivity makes the n‑back task widely used in neuroimaging studies (e.g., fNIRS, fMRI, EEG) to isolate brain regions engaged in working‑memory maintenance and manipulation. Meta‑analyses and fNIRS investigations (e.g., Shin et al., 2018; Yeung & Han, 2023) have shown that higher n‑back conditions produce greater activation in dorsal and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, reflecting elevated load, while medial frontal sites often show load‑independent activation linked to sustained attention.

In practice, participants respond (typically via a button press) when a match is detected. The task is often structured in blocks of trials or sets of stimuli with short inter‑stimulus intervals. Reaction time, accuracy, and neurophysiological measures are recorded together, allowing researchers to relate behavioral performance to neural correlates of working memory.

Sources
Shin, J., von Lühmann, A., Kim, D.-W., Mehnert, J., Hwang, H.-J., & Müller, K.-R. (2018). Simultaneous acquisition of EEG and NIRS during cognitive tasks for an open access dataset. Scientific Data, 5, 180003.

Yeung, M. K., & Han, Y. M. Y. (2023). Changes in task performance and frontal cortex activation within and over sessions during the n-back task. Scientific Reports, 13, 3363.

What is working memory?
assistant
General knowledge
General neuroscience background - not derived from this model's own findings.

Working memory is a cognitive system that temporarily holds and manipulates information needed for ongoing mental tasks such as reasoning, decision‑making, and learning. It is usually indexed in the laboratory with tasks that require participants to update or compare short‑term items, for example the n‑back task. In such paradigms, neural activity in frontal cortical regions—especially the dorsolateral and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex—is thought to support the load‑dependent maintenance and processing of information. Functional near‑infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) can non‑invasively probe this activity by measuring changes in oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated (HbR) haemoglobin that accompany local neuronal activation. Studies have found that, during higher working‑memory loads (e.g., 3‑back versus 1‑back), HbO levels increase in these lateral prefrontal sites, while medial frontal sites show activation that is less sensitive to load (Yeung & Han, 2023). HbO signals tend to be more reliable than HbR for detecting workload‑related changes, although both are influenced by physiological noise and motion artefacts, underscoring the importance of preprocessing and careful interpretation. Overall, working memory engages a network of frontal regions whose hemodynamic responses can be captured by fNIRS, providing a physiological correlate of the cognitive demands imposed by tasks that load short‑term memory.

Sources
Yeung, M. K., & Han, Y. M. Y. (2023). Changes in task performance and frontal cortex activation within and over sessions during the n-back task. Scientific Reports, 13, 3363.
